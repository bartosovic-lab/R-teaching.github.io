A normal distribution is a symmetric bell-shaped model. In a Q–Q plot, ordered observations are compared with the positions expected under that model. Approximate alignment with the line is compatible with normality; systematic bends or isolated tail points raise questions. Small samples are hard to diagnose. Inspect groups separately: pooling two different group means can produce a misleading shape.

par(mfrow=c(1,2))
qqnorm(first, main="Baseline"); qqline(first)
qqnorm(second, main="Acquisition"); qqline(second)

par(mfrow=c(1,1))
shapiro.test(first)
## 
##  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
## 
## data:  first
## W = 0.86713, p-value = 0.1752
shapiro.test(second)
## 
##  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
## 
## data:  second
## W = 0.9591, p-value = 0.8128

Shapiro–Wilk tests compatibility with a normal model. Its p-value measures how unusual the test statistic would be under that model and its assumptions. A large p-value does not prove normality, particularly with few animals. A small p-value does not automatically choose the scientific question or the next test.

3. What question does a test answer? — 30 minutes

Our null hypothesis for the mean comparison is that the two population means are equal. Welch’s t-test estimates a difference in means and allows the groups to have unequal variances. It needs independent animals; with small samples, strong skew and extreme values can make its results fragile. The analysis below compares Acquisition minus Baseline.

mean_difference <- mean(second) - mean(first)
mean_difference
## [1] 19.57663
welch <- t.test(second, first)
welch
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  second and first
## t = 10.029, df = 10.366, p-value = 1.163e-06
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  15.24791 23.90534
## sample estimates:
## mean of x mean of y 
## -58.53572 -78.11234
welch$conf.int
## [1] 15.24791 23.90534
## attr(,"conf.level")
## [1] 0.95
welch$p.value
## [1] 1.162881e-06

Read the output in this order: group means, estimated difference (above), 95% confidence interval, then p-value. Under its assumptions, a method producing 95% confidence intervals would cover the true difference in about 95% of repeated studies. This is not an interval containing 95% of individual animals. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true and does not measure the size or importance of an effect.

4. A different question: compare ranks — 20 minutes

Ranks replace measurements by their order: smallest, next smallest, and so on. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test compares the groups through ranks. It does not require normal measurements, but it still requires independent observations. With different shapes or spreads, it is not simply a test of medians. Under a common-shape location-shift model it can be interpreted as a location comparison. exact = FALSE requests the approximate calculation and accommodates ties.

rank_result <- wilcox.test(second, first, exact=FALSE)
rank_result
## 
##  Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction
## 
## data:  second and first
## W = 42, p-value = 0.003405
## alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

Keep both results. Choose your primary method because it addresses your question and fits the design, not because it gives the smaller p-value. Methods can disagree without either software command being broken.

5. Do two measurements travel together? — 15 minutes

Here we use only Baseline. An association across mixed groups can be driven by the groups themselves. Correlation requires two measurements from the same animal, not two unrelated columns lined up by spreadsheet position.

one_group <- study[study$group == "Baseline", ]
plot(one_group$value, one_group$companion, pch=19,
     xlab="Mean GABA reversal potential (mV)", ylab="Mean resting membrane potential (mV)")

cor.test(one_group$value, one_group$companion, method="pearson")
## 
##  Pearson's product-moment correlation
## 
## data:  one_group$value and one_group$companion
## t = -0.14109, df = 5, p-value = 0.8933
## alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -0.7790850  0.7244398
## sample estimates:
##         cor 
## -0.06297286
cor.test(one_group$value, one_group$companion, method="spearman", exact=FALSE)
## 
##  Spearman's rank correlation rho
## 
## data:  one_group$value and one_group$companion
## S = 58, p-value = 0.9394
## alternative hypothesis: true rho is not equal to 0
## sample estimates:
##         rho 
## -0.03571429

Pearson’s r describes a linear association and is sensitive to extreme values. Spearman’s rho describes an association in ranks that is monotonic (generally rising or falling). Both range from -1 to 1; zero does not rule out every curved relationship. Their inference needs independent animal pairs; the conventional Pearson test adds distributional assumptions. Inspect the scatterplot before interpreting a coefficient. Neither establishes causation.
