Notebook 1

Exercise 1: the original vector’s mean is 28.5; adding 26 gives 28. Assignment stores the four values under a name. Scores and scores are distinct objects.

Exercise 2: 20 rows, 3 columns. Teaching_ID identifies a row, Group is nominal, MMSE is a bounded discrete score. Equal vector lengths do not establish pairing. A score difference is useful descriptively but does not imply a calibrated equal change in overall cognition at every part of the scale.

Exercises 3–4: ten scores in each group. Group selection should agree with table(small$Group).

Notebook 2

The control histogram is concentrated near the upper score limit; AD scores are more spread and lower. Four-point bins conceal some integer detail without changing the observations. Neither a bell-like histogram nor a mean close to a median proves normality.

Boxplots show medians and hinges, not confidence intervals. Jitter is horizontal only; points preserve their score values. Mean bars conceal spread, sample size, and repeated values. A violin estimates a smooth density: with ten observations and tied scores, that smoothness can overstate how much is known about shape. Its width is not uncertainty. A caption should name MMSE points, the two groups, and ten observations each.

Notebook 3

Question and distributions

The initial target is the population mean-score difference under the independent-person teaching assumption, estimated by AD minus control. This is not a treatment experiment. A paired method would require matched observations, not equal counts.

shapiro.test(control)
##
##  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
##
## data:  control
## W = 0.82361, p-value = 0.02802
shapiro.test(ad)
##
##  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
##
## data:  ad
## W = 0.94219, p-value = 0.5776

Discuss the actual plots even if Shapiro does not reject at 0.05. The control values are discrete and constrained by a ceiling. Ten observations cannot provide strong reassurance about tails. Pooling the strongly separated groups answers the wrong normality question for this comparison.

Mean and rank comparisons

mean(ad) - mean(control)
## [1] -19.1
t.test(ad, control)
##
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
##
## data:  ad and control
## t = -22.153, df = 13.068, p-value = 9.486e-12
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -20.96162 -17.23838
## sample estimates:
## mean of x mean of y
##       9.0      28.1
wilcox.test(ad, control, exact = FALSE)
##
##  Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction
##
## data:  ad and control
## W = 0, p-value = 0.0001659
## alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

The observed mean difference is −19.1 points. The CI is for AD minus control, not for individual scores. A small p-value neither measures the probability the null hypothesis is true nor proves clinical importance or valid assumptions. Failure to reject is not equivalence.

Wilcoxon addresses rank ordering under its framework, not equality of means and not generally equality of medians without shape assumptions. Both methods require independent participants. A good answer identifies the target, acknowledges bounds/ties and small-sample approximation limits, and explains why a chosen primary method fits the question. Do not grade on whether students choose the instructor’s preferred method alone.

Both analyses here provide strong evidence for their respective questions. Agreement does not prove normality. If asked about disagreement, students should revisit shapes, influential values, assumptions, and the target, rather than selecting the smaller p-value. The rank-sum W and Shapiro W have different meanings.

The mean difference is about 0.430 recorded units, CI about 0.273–0.587. Individual ranges overlap but the uncertainty interval for a mean difference excludes zero. These are different quantities. The synthetic data’s physical units and generation design are undocumented; independent observations are an explicit teaching assumption.

Report rho and n = 136. The association is cross-sectional and cannot establish within-person cognitive decline with ageing. Discuss ceiling effects and group composition. Spearman’s approximate p-value handles ties explicitly; no Spearman CI is supplied by base R.

Worked correction and data audit

p <- c(A = 0.01, B = 0.03, C = 0.20)
data.frame(raw = p, adjusted = p.adjust(p, "bonferroni"))
##    raw adjusted
## A 0.01     0.03
## B 0.03     0.09
## C 0.20     0.60

All three prespecified comparisons form the example’s family. A and B fall below 0.05 before adjustment; only A does after it. Adjustment changes the evidence criterion, not the observed treatment differences. Selective reporting would conceal the number of tests tried.

The reference includes both methods for marking, although students choose one primary comparison. The score distributions have different shapes and a ceiling, especially in the Nondemented group. A Wilcoxon result should not be reported as a test of a median difference without additional assumptions. A Welch analysis answers the mean question but needs a discussion of its approximate inference on bounded distributions. Expect direction, relevant summaries, sample sizes, test statistic and p-value, and a confidence interval when using Welch.

Do not accept “Shapiro was significant, therefore Wilcoxon” as a complete justification. Ask what question the rank comparison answers. Do not accept “large n means assumptions no longer matter” either.

All options have 136 complete pairs. Award credit for choosing a linear or monotonic question and inspecting the plot, not for obtaining a particular coefficient. Pearson’s usual inference has assumptions; Spearman does not answer every nonlinear question and does not come with a base-R CI.

Colouring the scatterplot by group can reveal that between-group separation contributes to the pooled association. It does not itself adjust for group or establish causality. The student need only investigate one limitation, not run more subgroup tests.

Minimum submission feedback

Check for actual selected-test outputs; the blank template intentionally renders without doing inferential tests. Required work is one primary comparison, one correlation, and the separate correction explanation. If a student leaves selection messages, empty answer prompts, or only a screenshot of a result, ask them to complete the reasoning and code.

Good work connects the question, observation unit, assumptions, result and limitation. Allow short, direct answers. Partners may share code, but each should explain it. Ask each student to interpret one figure or predict the effect of changing an argument. LLM use itself is neither rewarded nor penalised; understanding and verification matter.

An overall discovery claim based on either of the two analyses needs multiplicity considered. Students can acknowledge the exploratory, unadjusted scope; an optional supplied two-test adjustment is available. Never encourage additional testing to find significance.
