4. Centre, spread and the middle half — 20 minutes

The mean gives each observation equal weight. The median is the middle of the ordered observations (the mean of the two middle ones when there is an even number). The sample standard deviation (SD) describes variation among observations, in the same units as the measurement. A larger SD means more variation; it does not by itself tell us how uncertain the mean is. Sample size also matters for that.

Where is the middle half?

Quartiles mark positions in ordered observations. Q1 is the 25th percentile, Q2 the median, and Q3 the 75th percentile. IQR = Q3 − Q1, in the measurement’s units. 6. How many bars tell the story? — 15 minutes

A histogram groups measurements into intervals called bins. Bar height counts observations inside each interval. A few broad bins can hide structure; many narrow bins can make a small sample look full of gaps. Neither setting adds information. The marks below the bars show where the actual observations lie. 7. Show the animals behind the average — 15 minutes

A boxplot puts the middle half of the observations inside a box. The line is the median. Whiskers extend to the most distant observations within 1.5 box lengths of the box; more distant observations are conventionally drawn separately. These points deserve investigation, not automatic deletion. Always look at the actual observations as well. The box is the middle half, not a confidence interval. R boxplot hinges and quantile() quartiles can differ slightly, especially in small samples.

In R’s plot formula BDNF ~ group, read ~ as “shown by group”. data = study tells R where those column names live. stripchart() adds individual observations. 9. Is a normal model a useful approximation? — 15 minutes

A normal distribution is a symmetric bell-shaped model. Real observations may be skewed (one tail longer), bounded or contain unusual values. Similar means and medians do not prove normality. Inspect groups separately: pooling groups can hide or invent shapes.

A Q–Q plot compares ordered observations with a normal model. Points roughly following the line are compatible with that model; bends and isolated tail points raise questions. Shapiro–Wilk supplies another check. Its p-value is not the probability that the data are normal. A large p-value does not prove normality; a small one does not choose the next test for you.

Inspect the other group

Run the Saline example. Add qqnorm(memantine_bdnf) followed by qqline(memantine_bdnf). Save shapiro.test(memantine_bdnf) as normal_check and display it. Explain the plots before reading the p-value.

10. Compare groups, then defend the claim — 30 minutes

Our question is about Memantine minus Saline BDNF signal. Predict the direction before testing. Welch’s t-test compares population means while allowing unequal variances. Independent animals are essential; strong skew and influential values can make small-sample inference fragile. The null hypothesis is equal population means. We do not need a preliminary equal-variance test.

11. Put a number on an association — 15 minutes

Use two measurements from the same mouse, with different mice independent. Pearson describes a linear association; Spearman describes monotonic rank association (generally rising or falling). Both range from −1 to +1. Zero does not exclude every curved relationship. Inspect the scatterplot before choosing; separate Shapiro tests do not select a correlation method. The usual Pearson test and interval additionally need an appropriate model for the paired measurements. Neither coefficient proves causation.

12. Three proteins, three chances for a small p-value — 15 minutes

Suppose we planned three treatment comparisons, one each for BDNF, pCREB and NR1 (an NMDA receptor subunit signal). They form a family of three tests. We supply their Welch p-values below; you do not need to write a loop or repeat three complete analyses. The assumptions and cohort limitations still apply to each comparison.

Bonferroni multiplies each p-value by the number of tests, capped at 1. This controls the chance of at least one false rejection in that family when the individual tests are valid, including when tests are dependent. It does not repair confounding. These three adjusted results do not cover additional correlation tests or undisclosed searches.

Keep every result in view

Run the supplied three comparisons. Append adjusted_p <- p.adjust(raw_p, method = "bonferroni") and data.frame(raw_p, adjusted_p). Compare both columns using a declared 0.05 threshold. A non-significant result still belongs in the report. The estimated group differences have not changed.

study <- read.csv("data/study.csv")
saline <- study[study$group == "Saline", ]
memantine <- study[study$group == "Memantine", ]
raw_p <- c(BDNF=t.test(memantine$BDNF, saline$BDNF)$p.value,
           pCREB=t.test(memantine$pCREB, saline$pCREB)$p.value,
           NR1=t.test(memantine$NR1, saline$NR1)$p.value)
